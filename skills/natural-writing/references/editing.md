## Protect the substance

Make a small fact ledger before a substantial rewrite: claims and their
certainty, actors, amounts, dates, conditions, requests and commitments.
Audit against it afterwards. Keep qualifiers that carry meaning. Do not turn
"could help" into "will", "usually" into "always", or an acknowledgment into
an apology. Keep the stated cause and eligibility rules. Preserve a useful
thank-you, invitation or benefit even when its first wording is formulaic.

## Remove the formula when it gets in the way

- Open with the actual point rather than "Great question" or "Let's dive in".
- Replace an unsupported benefit with the source's actual mechanism or outcome.
  Ask for the missing detail during the interview; never supply a made-up number.
- Cut a concluding slogan when the preceding facts already carry the conclusion.
- Keep a contrast when it corrects a real misconception. Repeated "not X but Y"
  framing is a reason to inspect the argument, not to delete its negative claim.
- Use direct verbs when they fit: "evaluate" instead of "perform an evaluation".
- Keep important transitions and connective words. Removing every "but" or "so"
  can flatten the writer's reasoning.
- Keep the same name for the same concept. Cycling between "platform", "solution"
  and "tool" can make one thing sound like several things.

Words such as "robust", "underscore", "transform", "powerful", and "delve" can
be literal or precise. Consider how they work in context. A single word never
establishes who wrote a passage or whether it needs an edit.

## Preserve texture

Natural contractions, everyday verbs, varied sentences and specific details
often help. Match the subject's samples rather than adding a colloquial pass.
Keep their jargon when it is the vocabulary their audience uses. Keep humor
when the writer actually supplies it. Preserve meaningful repetition, mixed
feelings, an aside, or an unusual word choice.

Dashes and semicolons are valid punctuation. Repeatedly using either for every
connection can be tiring; choose a different construction only where it reads
better. Avoid mechanically replacing dashes with semicolons. Do not manufacture
sentence-length variation to hit a statistic.

## Fit the format

| Context | What matters |
| --- | --- |
| Chat or thread reply | Answer or ask early; preserve real warmth. |
| Business email | Reason for writing, actual ask, appropriate greeting and sign-off. |
| Public announcement | What changes, who it affects, scope, dates, next action. |
| Social post or article | The subject's reasoning and examples; the ending earns its place. |
| Documentation | Precise terms, usable navigation, steps, code and necessary caveats. |

These are starting points, not universal structures. Current instructions and
the subject's genre-specific examples take precedence.
